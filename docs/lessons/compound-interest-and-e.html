Investigation \(7\) · Preparation

Build interest from repeated growth

Connect the rate per period with the number of periods.

Follow the balance one period at a time

The investigation’s starting account

Invest \(\$1{,}000\) at a nominal annual interest rate of \(8\%\). Compare different compounding schedules. Keep the money in the account, with no extra deposits, withdrawals, or fees.

Build the compound-interest formula

  1. Write the advertised nominal annual rate as a decimal: \(r=0.08\).
  2. If interest is compounded \(n\) times per year, the rate per period is \(r/n\), so the period factor is \(1+r/n\).
  3. After \(t\) years there are \(nt\) periods. Apply the same factor that many times.

Periodic compounding

\(A_n(t)=P\left(1+\dfrac{r}{n}\right)^{nt}\).

Here \(P\) is the starting balance in dollars, \(r\) is the nominal annual rate as a decimal, \(n\) is the number of compounding periods per year, and \(t\) is elapsed time in years.

Interest is earned on the current balance, including previously earned interest. Quarterly compounding at \(8\%\) uses a \(2\%\) rate each quarter; the annual percent yield will be slightly greater than \(8\%\).

This first tool plots completed crediting periods. Later comparisons use full years or completed quarters. These are idealized mathematical accounts with fixed rates.

Full comparison table

Advertised rate, annual factor, and APY

Annual growth factor:

\(g_n=\left(1+\dfrac{r}{n}\right)^n\).

Annual percent yield:

\(\mathrm{APY}=100(g_n-1)\%\).

The nominal rate \(r\) determines the rate per compounding period. APY measures the actual percentage increase over one year in this model.

Then \(A_n(5)=1000g_n^5\). The five-year total percent change is \(100(g_n^5-1)\%\), which is different from APY.

More frequent compounding gives a larger balance here, but the gains get smaller. The balance approaches \(1000e^{0.4}\); it does not grow without bound as the compounding frequency increases.

How the table differences are reported

“APY change” means a difference in percentage points between consecutive schedules. For example, quarterly APY minus yearly APY is approximately \(8.243216-8=0.243216\) percentage points.

Compare the source’s annual rates

For rates \(5\%\), \(6\%\), \(7\%\), and \(8\%\), compare \(10{,}000\) compounding periods with the continuous limit.

Where the number comes from

Temporarily start with \(\$1\) and a nominal annual rate of \(100\%\). This exaggerated rate makes the pattern visible. Compounding \(n\) times gives

\(E_n=\left(1+\dfrac1n\right)^n\).

With annual compounding, the result is \(2\). With two periods it is \((1+1/2)^2=2.25\). Increasing \(n\) produces larger values that settle near a fixed number.

\(e\) is the limiting value of \((1+1/n)^n\) as \(n\) increases without bound.

\(e\approx2.718281828459\ldots\)

It is an irrational constant, like \(\pi\). It is not an interest rate and it does not depend on the principal.

For a general nominal annual rate \(r\), the annual factor approaches \(e^r\):

\(\left(1+\dfrac rn\right)^n\longrightarrow e^r\).

The graph provides numerical evidence for this limit. A finite number of compounding periods gives an approximation, even when rounding makes it look identical to the limit.

Investigation \(7\) · Explore and apply

Build the continuous-compounding formula

Use an annual factor to describe the account at any elapsed time.

Explore a continuously compounded account

The source compares \(\$1{,}000\) placed in four accounts at nominal annual rates of \(5\%\), \(6\%\), \(7\%\), and \(8\%\), with continuous compounding. Let \(t\) be years since the initial investment.

From an annual factor to a time formula

  1. Continuous compounding gives annual growth factor \(g=e^r\).
  2. After \(t\) years, the factor from the start is \(g^t=(e^r)^t\).
  3. Multiply the initial amount by that factor: \(A(t)=P(e^r)^t=Pe^{rt}\).

Continuous compounding

\(A(t)=Pe^{rt}\).

\(\text{annual factor}=e^r,\qquad \mathrm{APY}=100(e^r-1)\%\).

At \(t=0\), \(A(0)=P\). At \(r=0.08\), the annual factor is approximately \(1.083287\), so APY is approximately \(8.328707\%\).

Continuous compounding is the limit of increasing frequency while holding the nominal rate fixed. For this positive-rate model it gives the largest balance in that comparison.

If time is measured in months \(m\), convert years to \(t=m/12\): \(A(m)=Pe^{rm/12}\). The rate and time units must agree.

Compare balances and doubling intervals

Invest \(\$1{,}500\) at a nominal annual rate of \(7\%\), compounded continuously. Find the value after \(6\) years and the time required to double. Then compare with quarterly compounding at the same rate.

Move the start: the same interval doubles the current balance.

Green curve: continuousBlue dots: quarterly crediting datesOrange: selected value or doubling endpoints

Doubling depends on the rate, not the start

The continuous model is \(A(t)=1500e^{0.07t}\). To double the starting balance, solve \(1500e^{0.07T}=3000\).

\(e^{0.07T}=2\), so the graph reaches twice the starting amount after approximately \(9.902\) years.

For any starting time \(s\),

\(\dfrac{A(s+T)}{A(s)}=e^{0.07T}=2\).

Move the doubling interval along the graph. Its width stays fixed even though its endpoint balances increase.

Optional exact expression using logarithms

\(T=\ln(2)/0.07\approx9.90210258\) years. The natural logarithm undoes an exponential with base \(e\). You can also estimate \(T\) from the graph without using logarithms.

With quarterly compounding, use \(Q(t)=1500(1+0.07/4)^{4t}\). At \(t=6\), both models use the same principal, rate, and elapsed time; only the compounding rule changes.
