01 / Identify quantities

Which quantities change together?

Definition · Constant rate of change

Two quantities x and y have a constant rate of change if the ratio of their corresponding changes has the same value over every interval with Δx ≠ 0.

m = Δy / Δx   ⇔   Δy = mΔx

The units of m are output units per input unit. Choose which quantity is the input before interpreting the rate.

Recall: a quantity is an attribute of an object or situation that can be measured or counted using a specified unit. Define each variable using what is measured, the reference point, the direction when relevant, and the unit.

02 / Build the rate

Building a constant rate of change

Start with two measurements. Compare their changes. Explain the “per,” then write a formula that predicts a value.

Specify what each number measures.

A rate compares changes in two quantities. Before calculating, identify both quantities, their units, and their reference points.

Filling a swimming pool

Scenario from M2I3 · Exercise \(2\)

The owner of a swimming pool wants to know the rate at which the water’s depth is changing with respect to time. When he begins filling the pool at \(12{:}00\) p.m., there is already some water in the pool. At \(2{:}00\) p.m., the depth of the water is \(21\) inches, and when it is \(5{:}00\) p.m., the depth of the water is \(36\) inches.

Modeling assumption for this guide: water depth increases at a constant rate over the times we consider, while the pool is still filling and has not overflowed.

The input

Let \(t\) be elapsed time in hours since noon, when filling begins. Thus \(t=0\) at noon.

The output

Let \(d\) be water depth in inches above the bottom of the pool.

The question

How much does depth change per hour of elapsed time?

Record paired values.

Observation\(t\) (hours since noon)\(d\) (inches)
Earlier\(2\)\(21\)
Later\(5\)\(36\)

Each row describes the pool at one instant. The two rows are not changes yet.

Subtract to find corresponding changes.

Use the same interval and the same direction for both quantities: later minus earlier.

From \(t=2\) to \(t=5\) hours, depth changes from \(d=21\) to \(d=36\) inches.

\(\Delta t=t_{\mathrm{final}}-t_{\mathrm{initial}}\)
\(\Delta d=d_{\mathrm{final}}-d_{\mathrm{initial}}\)

The symbol \(\Delta\) means “change in.” A value describes one instant; a change compares two instants.

The final depth is \(36\) inches. The increase over this interval is \(15\) inches.

We have found \(15\) inches of depth increase over \(3\) hours. To get a rate per hour, we need one more operation.

Divide to express change per input unit.

“Per hour” asks how much depth change corresponds to one hour of time change. Divide the output change by the input change.

\(\frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t}=\frac{15\ \mathrm{in}}{3\ \mathrm{hr}}=5\ \frac{\mathrm{in}}{\mathrm{hr}}\)

The depth increases by \(5\) inches for each \(1\)-hour increase in time, under the constant-rate assumption.

Why division?

Scale the paired changes \((3,15)\) by \(\frac13\). They become \((1,5)\): one hour paired with five inches.

Why this order?

Depth with respect to time means depth change on top and time change on the bottom.

What do the units say?

\(\mathrm{in}/\mathrm{hr}\) tells us the output unit per input unit. The order is part of the meaning.

Check the meaning of the quotient.

Why not divide the values \(36/5\)?

The depth \(36\) includes water that was already in the pool at noon. It is not the depth increase over \(5\) hours. A rate compares changes: \((36-21)/(5-2)\).

The reverse quotient is \(\frac{3}{15}=\frac15\) hour per inch. It answers a different question: how much time change corresponds to one inch of depth change?

What the observations establish: \(5\) inches per hour is the average rate over this interval. Two observations alone do not establish a constant rate between them or elsewhere. The constant-rate assumption lets us extend this ratio to every interval in the model.

Make the same ratio hold over every interval.

Constant does not mean the quantities stop changing. It means that their corresponding changes always have the same ratio.

For input \(x\) and output \(y\), a constant rate of change means there is one number \(m\) such that

\(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}=m\quad\text{whenever }\Delta x\ne0.\)

For the pool, \(\Delta d/\Delta t=5\) inches per hour for every nonzero time interval in the model.

These intermediate depths are predictions of the constant-rate model. Choose two times to compare the changes.

A longer interval

Doubling the time change doubles the depth change. Their ratio stays \(5\).

A reversed interval

Both changes reverse sign. Their ratio is still positive: \((-15)/(-3)=5\).

A zero interval

When \(\Delta t=0\), the ratio is \(0/0\), which is undefined. This does not make the model’s rate zero.

Build the formula one step at a time.

First write a rule for the changes. Then use a known value to recover the final value.

From the rate to a prediction

Known pool observation: \((t_0,d_0)=(2,21)\). Here the subscript \(0\) labels our chosen reference point; it does not mean that the reference time is zero.

Start with the constant ratio
\(\frac{\Delta d}{\Delta t}=5\qquad(\Delta t\ne0)\)

Depth change per time change is \(5\) inches per hour.

Multiply both sides by the time change
\(\Delta d=5\Delta t\)

This gives a depth change. The units agree: \((\mathrm{in}/\mathrm{hr})\times\mathrm{hr}=\mathrm{in}\).

Write each change from the known point
\(d-21=5(t-2)\)

The new time is \(t\), so its change from \(2\) hours is \(t-2\). The new depth is \(d\), so its change from \(21\) inches is \(d-21\).

Add the reference depth to recover the new depth
\(d=21+5(t-2)\)

New value = reference value + change. Adding the change to the known depth gives the new depth.

Expand and combine
\(d=21+5t-10=11+5t\)

The model predicts \(11\) inches at noon. The term \(5t\) is the depth increase since noon; \(d\) is the total depth.

The general form
\(\Delta y=m\Delta x\)
\(y-y_0=m(x-x_0)\)
\(y=y_0+m(x-x_0)\)

Use a known pair \((x_0,y_0)\) and the constant rate \(m\). When \(x=x_0\), the final formula correctly returns \(y=y_0\), even though the change ratio is undefined for that zero interval.

How does this become \(y=mx+b\)?
\(y=mx+(y_0-mx_0)=mx+b\)

Thus \(b=y_0-mx_0\). It is the model’s output at input \(0\), when that input is meaningful in the situation. Only when \(b=0\) do we have \(y=mx\), a proportional relationship between the values.

Keep the rate, change, and value separate.

A method you can reuse

  1. Define the input and output with their units and reference points.
  2. Compute corresponding changes in a consistent direction.
  3. Divide output change by input change to find the rate and its units.
  4. Establish or state the constant-rate assumption before using that rate on other intervals.
  5. Multiply to find a change, then add it to a known value.

03 / Changes, rates & initial values

A rate relates changes.

Δy = mΔx
yfinal = yinitial + mΔx

The rate m, the change Δy, and the final value y are different quantities. If a known point is (x₀, y₀), then y = y₀ + m(x − x₀). The input change is x − x₀, not necessarily x.

Minutes per mile versus miles per minute

During the constant-pace segment, Δt = 4Δd and Δd = ¼Δt. The reciprocal rates are 4 min/mi and 0.25 mi/min. Time spent warming up is not part of the elapsed time within this segment.

Let t be minutes since the ride began, including the two-minute warm-up, and let d be miles traveled after the warm-up. On the constant-pace segment, d = (t − 2)/4, for 2 ≤ t ≤ 22. The distance ridden during the warm-up is not given.

From two observations to a formula

Compare with the derivation

Δt = 5 − 2 = 3 hours and Δd = 36 − 21 = 15 inches, so m = 15/3 = 5 in/hr. From the known point (2, 21), d = 21 + 5(t − 2) = 11 + 5t.

The initial water depth is 11 inches. The increase since noon is 5t inches. Depth and depth increase are not interchangeable.

05 / Proportionality & word problems

Are the changes proportional, or the values?

Definition · Proportional relationship

The values x and y are proportional when y = mx for a constant m. For x ≠ 0, the ratio of values y/x stays constant. Its graph passes through (0, 0) when zero is in the domain.

A general linear relationship y = b + mx has proportional changes, Δy = mΔx. Its values are proportional only when b = 0 (on a nontrivial interval).

Resize a picture without changing its shape.

Lengths and area scale differently. Width and height are each proportional to \(k\), so their ratio stays constant. Area is multiplied by \(k^2\): doubling both lengths makes the area \(4\) times as large.

Remaining length versus length burned

Let t be hours since lighting, r the remaining length in inches, and b the length burned in inches. Time starts when the candle is lit; remaining length is measured from the candle’s base to its current top.

Compare your explanation

For the 19-inch candle, r = 19 − 2.5t and Δr = −2.5Δt during burning. The changes have a fixed ratio, but r/t is not constant and r(0) = 19. The length burned b = 2.5t is proportional to time. The whole candle burns away after 19/2.5 = 7.6 hours.

For the 17-inch candle, r = 17 − 1.8t, b = 1.8t, and Δr = −1.8Δt. Its burn time is 17/1.8 ≈ 9.44 hours. These formulas apply only until the candle is fully burned.
