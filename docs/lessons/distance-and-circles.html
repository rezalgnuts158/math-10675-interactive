Exercise \(1\) · The situation

Which distance determines reception?

You are considering renting a house at D Street and \(23^{\mathrm{rd}}\) Avenue. Your provider’s closest cell tower is at B Street and \(20^{\mathrm{th}}\) Avenue. Neighboring streets and avenues are each \(1\) mile apart. A house farther than \(3.5\) miles from the tower will not have good reception.

Using the lower-left corner of the supplied map as \((0,0)\), the tower is \(C=(2,1)\) and the house is \(P=(4,4)\).

The coordinates describe locations. We need to turn their differences into a distance.

The diagonal joins the two locations directly. The next step will build a right triangle around it, using distances we can read from the grid.
Exercise \(1a\) · Build the triangle

Read the horizontal and vertical separations.

Go horizontally from the tower to the point directly below or above the house, then vertically to the house. These perpendicular segments are the legs of a right triangle.

Changes have signs; lengths do not. The horizontal change is \(\Delta x=x-h\), but the horizontal leg length is \(|x-h|\). Likewise, the vertical leg length is \(|y-k|\).

Try moving P to the left of or below C. A coordinate change may become negative; its corresponding leg length stays nonnegative.

Exercise \(1b\) · Use Pythagoras

The diagonal is the hypotenuse.

A right triangle connects the unknown straight-line distance \(d\) to the two known leg lengths.

Square the leg lengths and add

\(d^2=(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2\)

Squaring the absolute lengths gives the same result as squaring the signed changes.

Take the nonnegative square root

\(d=\sqrt{(\Delta x)^2+(\Delta y)^2}\)

Distance is nonnegative, so we use the principal square root, not a negative distance.

miles

Why not add the two leg lengths?

\(2+3=5\) miles is the right-angle street route. The direct distance is \(\sqrt{2^2+3^2}=\sqrt{13}\approx3.606\) miles. Adding lengths answers a different question.

Exercise \(1c\)–\(1g\) · Generalize

Replace particular coordinates with any two points.

For \(C=(h,k)\) and \(P=(x,y)\),

\(d=\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}.\)

Subtract coordinates along the same axis. Square the differences, add them, and take the nonnegative square root.

The source uses \((h_1,v_1)\) for a general tower; here we use \((h,k)\), the same center notation used for a circle.

The second tower is at \((6,2)\), and the house is still at \((4,4)\). From tower to house, the changes are \(4-6=-2\) and \(4-2=2\).

miles

A zero horizontal or vertical separation collapses one leg. The formula still gives the remaining straight-line separation; if the points coincide, it gives \(d=0\).

Exercise \(2\) · Hold distance fixed

What shape do equal distances make?

A cell tower is at \(C=(-2,4)\), with coverage radius \(3\) miles. East is the positive horizontal direction and north is the positive vertical direction. Every point exactly \(3\) miles from the tower lies on the service boundary.

Now C is fixed and P can move, but its distance from C must stay \(3\) miles. Drag P or move the sweep slider to trace those possible locations.

A circle is the set of all points at a fixed distance from a fixed center.

The fixed distance is the radius \(r\).

Watch the triangle change shape. Its two legs vary, but their squared lengths keep the same sum because its hypotenuse stays \(r\).

From fixed distance to an equation

Set the distance equal to the radius.

Use the distance formula

\(d=\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}\)

Require the point to be on the boundary

\(\sqrt{(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2}=r\)

The varying point must stay exactly \(r\) units from \((h,k)\).

Square both nonnegative sides

\((x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2\)

Conversely, this equation gives distance \(r\) when \(r>0\). It describes precisely the circle, not just a few sample points.

Current circle

Move the center or change the radius. P stays on the boundary, and the equation updates with the same geometry.

Read the signs as subtraction. \(x-(-2)=x+2\), so \((x+2)^2\) corresponds to a center’s horizontal coordinate of \(-2\). The right side is the radius squared.

Exercise \(2a\)–\(2b\) · Use the circle

Find the boundary point southeast of the tower.

The tower is at \((-2,4)\), its radius is \(3\) miles, and a point \((x,2)\) southeast of the tower lies on the service boundary. What is \(x\)?

\((x+2)^2+(2-4)^2=9\)
\((x+2)^2=5\)
\(x=-2\pm\sqrt5\)

The horizontal line \(y=2\) meets the circle twice. “Southeast” means right of and below the center, so we need \(x>-2\).

Why keep only one of the two solutions?

\(x=-2+\sqrt5\approx0.236\) is east of \(-2\); \(x=-2-\sqrt5\approx-4.236\) is west of \(-2\). Both points are south of the center because \(2<4\). Only the plus choice is southeast.

Boundary versus covered region

Drag P freely to test a location. In this idealized coverage model:

Boundary: \((x+2)^2+(y-4)^2=9\).

Inside: \((x+2)^2+(y-4)^2<9\).

Inside or on the boundary: \((x+2)^2+(y-4)^2\le9\).

The connection: the distance formula computes how far apart two points are. The circle equation requires that distance to equal one fixed radius.
