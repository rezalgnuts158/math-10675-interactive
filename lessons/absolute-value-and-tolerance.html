How close is close enough?

A carpenter needs to cut a wooden board to a length of \(7.6\) inches with a tolerance of \(0.1\) inch. The actual length after the cut can be within \(0.1\) inch of \(7.6\) inches and still be usable.

Let \(x\) be the board’s length after cutting, measured in inches.

There are usable lengths on both sides of the target. “Within” includes a board whose error is exactly the allowed tolerance.

Exercise \(2\) · Distance and absolute value

Keep the size of the change; ignore its direction.

Absolute value measures magnitude. On a number line, \(|x-h|\) is the distance between \(x\) and the reference value \(h\).

The signed change \(x-h\) may be negative, zero, or positive. Its absolute value is always nonnegative.

\(|x-h|=|h-x|\)

Let \(x\) represent a quantity’s value. Choose values of \(x\) within \(4.5\) units of \(16\).

Which inequality describes the allowed signed changes?

Three descriptions of the same set:

Distance: \(|x-16|\le4.5\).

Signed change: \(-4.5\le x-16\le4.5\).

Quantity’s value: \(11.5\le x\le20.5\).

Connect this with the general definition

For a positive distance \(c\), \(|x-h|=c\) means exactly \(c\) units away; \(|x-h|c\) means more than \(c\) units away.

For example, \(|x-11|\le3\) describes \(8\le x\le14\). Try this in the exploration controls.

Exercise \(3\) · Direction matters

Positive change is a one-sided condition.

Let \(x\) represent a quantity’s value. We want changes in \(x\) away from \(5\) that are positive but no more than \(3\) units.

“Positive” tells us which side of \(5\) to use. “No more than \(3\)” tells us how far to go. The reference value itself has zero change, which is not positive.
