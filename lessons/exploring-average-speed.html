Exploring Average Speed

Same distance. Same time. What constant speed would do the job?

Work through each scenario. Try the questions before opening the explanations. Written reasoning is for discussion and is not automatically graded.
Investigation 5 · Exercise 1 of 6

What does an average speed mean?

Laura’s concert begins in 3 hours. Haley is 282 miles away.

Compare reasoning

Consider departure time, stops, traffic, the route, and reasonable driving speeds. A constant-speed model lets us estimate the required average.

mi/h

Show explanation

\(\frac{282}{3}=94\) mi/h. This is the constant speed that would cover the same distance in the same time.

Compare reasoning

An average of 94 mi/h would be needed for the entire trip. Any time spent stopped or moving below that rate would require faster travel elsewhere. This is not a reasonable driving plan.

Average speed describes an entire interval. It does not say the car maintained that speed at every moment.
Investigation 5 · Exercise 2 of 6

Use an average to make a prediction

Isabel and Miles must travel 145 miles. They left 3 hours before the wedding, but covered only 90 miles in the first 2 hours on icy roads.

mi/h

Show explanation

\(\frac{90}{2}=45\) mi/h.

miles

Show explanation

\(45\times3=135\) miles. They would still be 10 miles short, so they would not arrive on time.

mi/h

Show explanation

\(145-90=55\) miles remain, with \(3-2=1\) hour available. The required average is \(\frac{55}{1}=55\) mi/h.

Compare reasoning

It is the constant speed needed to cover the remaining 55 miles in the remaining hour. It is different from the average over the whole trip.

Investigation 5 · Exercise 3 of 6

What information do we need?

An officer beside a road has no radar gun. How could the officer estimate a car’s speed?

Compare reasoning

Measure the time taken to travel a known road distance. Divide distance traveled by elapsed time, with consistent units. If that average exceeds the speed limit, the car exceeded the limit at some point. An average below the limit does not rule out a brief period of speeding.

Try a measurement Added exploration

Convert ft/s to mi/h by multiplying by \(\frac{3600}{5280}\).

Compare reasoning

No. It determines an average over the measured interval. Different patterns of motion can cover the same distance in the same time.

Investigation 5 · Exercise 4 of 6

From a curved graph to a constant-rate model

A car’s distance north of a crosswalk is \(d(t)=t^2+3.5\), in feet, with t in seconds.

feet

Show explanation

\(d(1) = 4.5\) and \(d(4) = 19.5\), so \(\Delta d=19.5-4.5=15\) feet.

Compare reasoning

Useful points are \((0, 3.5)\), \((1, 4.5)\), \((2, 7.5)\), \((3, 12.5)\), and \((4, 19.5)\). A point (t, d) gives the distance north at time t. The horizontal change is 3 seconds and the vertical change is 15 feet.

Show explanation

From 1 to 2 seconds the car travels 3 feet; from 2 to 3 it travels 5 feet; from 3 to 4 it travels 7 feet. Equal times give unequal distances.

ft/s

Show explanation

\(\frac{19.5-4.5}{4-1}=\frac{15}{3}=5\) ft/s. This constant speed covers the same 15 feet in the same 3 seconds.

Show explanation

The curve gives the actual position. The secant gives a hypothetical constant-rate motion with the same starting and ending positions. They agree at the two endpoints.

Investigation 5 · Exercise 5 of 6

Keep track of the starting position

A car’s distance south of an intersection is \(d(t)=2t^2-8t+8=2(t-2)^2\), in feet, for \(t \ge 0\).

feet

Show explanation

\(d(2)=2(2)^2-8(2)+8=0\). After 2 seconds the car is at the intersection.

feet

Show explanation

\(d(5)=50-40+8=18\). After 5 seconds the car is 18 feet south of the intersection.

ft/s

Show explanation

\(\frac{18-0}{5-2}=6\) ft/s, the constant speed needed to cover 18 feet in 3 seconds.

Explore the turn and the interval

Move the endpoints to compare the curve with a constant-rate model.

Compare reasoning

From 0 to 2 seconds the car moves from 8 feet south to the intersection. From 2 to 5 seconds it moves away to 18 feet south. The secant from \((2, 0)\) to \((5, 18)\) has slope 6 ft/s.

feet

Show explanation

There are \(13-5=8\) additional seconds. It travels \(6\times8=48\) additional feet, so its position is \(18+48=66\) feet south. This uses the new constant-speed assumption, not the original quadratic beyond \(t = 5\).

Average rate of change

\(m=\frac{f(t_2)-f(t_1)}{t_2-t_1}\)

The slope of the secant is the constant rate that produces the same change in output over the same interval.
Investigation 5 · Exercise 6 of 6

Compare averages over successive intervals

A car’s distance east of an intersection is \(d(t)=t^2-2t\), in feet. Use the stated intervals and include units.

ft/s

Show explanation

\(d(2) = 0\) and \(d(6) = 24.\) Average speed = \(\frac{24-0}{6-2}=6\) ft/s.

ft/s

Show explanation

\(d(6) = 24\) and \(d(10) = 80.\) Average speed = \(\frac{80-24}{10-6}=14\) ft/s.

ft/s

Show explanation

\(d(10) = 80\) and \(d(11) = 99.\) Average speed = \(\frac{99-80}{11-10}=19\) ft/s.

Compare reasoning

The average speed increases over these successive intervals: 6, 14, then 19 ft/s. For this quadratic, the car is speeding up throughout the stated interval.

Takeaway

Find the change in output, find the change in input, and divide. Explain the result as a constant rate over that particular interval.

Average speed uses total distance traveled. A secant slope uses net change in position. They agree for the forward-moving intervals used in these exercises; an interval containing a reversal requires care.
