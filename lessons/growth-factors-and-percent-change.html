The original scenario

Place \(2\) pennies on the first square of a chessboard. Double this to \(4\) on the second square, then \(8\) on the third. Continue doubling across all \(64\) squares.

Each step multiplies the previous amount by the same factor.

\(P(n)=2\cdot2^{n-1}=2^n,\quad n\in\{1,2,\ldots,64\}\).

There are \(63\) doublings after the first square. The exponent is \(n\) because the starting amount is already \(2\). The formula names the pennies on square \(n\), not the total on the board.

Constant ratios, not constant differences

The chessboard has integer inputs. A continuous exponential model also accepts fractional and negative inputs. For \(f(x)=2^x\), the graph is defined for all real \(x\), and every output is positive.

For equal changes in input, an exponential function has the same ratio of outputs.

\(\dfrac{F(x+\Delta x)}{F(x)}=b^{\Delta x}\), when \(F(x)=ab^x\) with \(a>0,\ b>0\).

For a one-unit step the ratio is \(b\). Move the interval to a noninteger starting input: the same multiplier still works. A two-unit step has factor \(b^2\), not \(2b\).

In \(2^x\) and \(3^x\), outputs grow without bound to the right and approach \(0\) to the left. For \(6(2/3)^x\), those directions reverse. None of these curves reaches \(0\).

“Percent of” and “percent change”

Factor: \(b=\dfrac{\text{new}}{\text{old}}\).

Percent of old: \(100b\%\).

Percent change: \(\dfrac{\text{new}-\text{old}}{\text{old}}\cdot100\%=(b-1)100\%\).

If the signed percent change is \(r\%\), then \(b=1+r/100\). Growth has \(b>1\); decay has \(0<b<1\). A factor of \(1\) gives a constant function.

In \(F(x)=ab^x\), the initial value is \(F(0)=a\). The one-unit factor is \(b\). These numbers answer different questions.

Original example: \(g(p)=1.578(0.68)^p\). The initial value is \(1.578\), and each one-unit increase in \(p\) retains \(68\%\) of the previous output.

Build a model from a story

House scenario

At the beginning of \(2020\), a house is worth \(\$280{,}000\). Assume its value grows by \(16\%\) per year. Model the value \(t\) years after that starting time.

\(H(t)=280{,}000(1.16)^t\).

Each year adds \(16\%\) of that year’s starting value. The dollar increase changes even though the percent increase stays fixed.

Population scenario

A city has \(1.4\) million people at the beginning of \(2020\). Assume the population decreases by \(2.1\%\) per year.

\(C(t)=1.4(0.979)^t\), with output in millions of people.

These are the investigation’s assumed models, not statements about actual historical prices or populations. Extending the house model to \(2050\) assumes the same rate continues for \(30\) years. That calculation alone does not justify the assumption.

Find the factor before the initial value

  1. Compare outputs with a known input gap.
  2. For a one-unit gap, \(b=y_2/y_1\). For a gap \(k\), the ratio is \(b^k\).
  3. Recover the initial value: \(a=y/b^x\).
  4. Write \(F(x)=ab^x\) and check against the data.

In Exercise \(9\), the first listed input is \(1\), so \(260\) is not the initial value. Keep \(a=260/1.15\) until the end.
